# Literature

What was already known before this study, who established it, on what data, and what it leaves open.

## 1. The origin: the co-holding puzzle

Gross, D. B., & Souleles, N. S. (2002). Do liquidity constraints and interest rates matter for consumer behavior? Evidence from credit card data. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 117*(1), 149–185. https://doi.org/10.1162/003355302753399472

Telyukova, I. A. (2013). Household need for liquidity and the credit card debt puzzle. *The Review of Economic Studies, 80*(3), 1148–1177. https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdt001

Many households carry expensive credit card debt while holding cash that could pay it off. Using the 1995 SCF, Gross and Souleles found that about one-third of card borrowers held more than a month's income in liquid assets, and that card debt responds strongly to interest rates. Telyukova found 27% of households co-holding in the 2001 SCF and showed that the need for cash, for spending a card can't cover, explains much of it.

This is where our one-month threshold comes from, and the basis for our interest-rate hypothesis.

## 2. Why people co-hold

Bertaut, C. C., Haliassos, M., & Reiter, M. (2009). Credit card debt puzzles and debt revolvers for self control. *Review of Finance, 13*(4), 657–692.

Gathergood, J., & Weber, J. (2014). Self-control, financial literacy & the co-holding puzzle. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 107*(Part B), 455–469.

Druedahl, J., & Jørgensen, C. N. (2018). Precautionary borrowing and the credit card debt puzzle. *Quantitative Economics, 9*(2), 785–823.

Gorbachev, O., & Luengo-Prado, M. J. (2019). The credit card debt puzzle: The role of preferences, credit access risk, and financial literacy. *The Review of Economics and Statistics, 101*(2), 294–309.

Greene, C., & Stavins, J. (2023). Credit card debt puzzle: Liquid assets to pay household bills. *International Review of Economics, 70*(4), 503–535.

Vihriälä, E. (2025). Intra-household frictions, anchoring, and the credit card debt puzzle. *The Review of Economics and Statistics, 107*(2), 510–522. https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_01264

Proposed explanations include self-control (keeping card balances to limit spending), precaution (debt preserves future access to credit), bills that must be paid in cash, and couples managing money separately. Each has support. None is tested in our study, and our data cannot say *why* co-holders answer as they do.

## 3. The personal finance literature

Ricaldi, L. C., Martin, T. K., & Huston, S. J. (2022). Financial literacy and its impact on the credit card debt puzzle. *Financial Services Review, 30*, 107–124. https://doi.org/10.61190/fsr.v30i2.3477

George, E. E., & Leszczyszyn, N. (2021). Overconfidence and the credit card debt puzzle. *Journal of Financial Counseling and Planning, 32*(2), 252–265. https://doi.org/10.1891/JFCP-20-00004

Both use the 2016 SCF. Ricaldi and colleagues found co-holders more likely than convenience users to have lower financial literacy. George and Leszczyszyn found about 31% of households co-holding, and overconfident consumers 20–40% more likely to co-hold.

## 4. Asking people what they would do with extra money

Fuster, A., Kaplan, G., & Zafar, B. (2021). What would you do with $500? Spending responses to gains, losses, news, and loans. *The Review of Economic Studies, 88*(4), 1760–1795.

Coibion, O., Gorodnichenko, Y., & Weber, M. (2020). How did U.S. consumers use their stimulus payments? NBER Working Paper 27693. (A working paper; no journal version found.)

Cookson, J. A., Gilje, E. P., & Heimer, R. Z. (2022). Shale shocked: Cash windfalls and household debt repayment. *Journal of Financial Economics, 146*(3), 905–931.

Hypothetical windfall questions are informative, and liquid wealth is their most reliable predictor (Fuster et al.). With real 2020 stimulus payments, people without a cash cushion were no more likely to spend but much more likely to pay down debt (Coibion et al.). Real cash windfalls from oil and gas leases were used partly to repay debt (Cookson et al.).

Leaves open: none of these compare families who already hold cash with those who don't, among card borrowers, with full balance sheets.

## 5. The 2025 SCF windfall question

Aladangady, A., Bricker, J., Chang, A. C., Goodman, S., & Li, G. (2026). Heterogeneity in the marginal propensity to consume among U.S. households. *FEDS Notes*, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System. (A FEDS Note, not a peer-reviewed article.)

Federal Reserve Board staff (2026). Measuring spending in the Survey of Consumer Finances. *FEDS Notes*. (A FEDS Note.)

The Fed's own analysis of the new question: families would spend 22%, save 47%, and pay down debt with 30% of an extra month of income, and about 47% would spend none of it. The note studies the *spending* share by liquidity, income, and literacy. Its authors say a working paper will follow.

Leaves open: the *debt* share, card borrowers, and co-holding.

## 6. Asking co-holders, and telling them

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (2020). Experiment suggests people pay down debt but keep savings cushion. CFPB blog. (Not peer reviewed.)

Batista, R., et al. Disclosing the costs of co-holding liquid assets and high-interest debt has limited impact on behavior. Working paper. (Journal status not confirmed.)

In an online experiment, people deciding for a fictional borrower put more than half of his savings toward his card debt, while keeping a cushion. At an Australian bank, telling real co-holders what co-holding costs them changed their behavior little.

Leaves open: what people say about *their own* money, on *their own* balance sheets.

## 7. What we claim, and what we do not

**We do not claim:**
- to have discovered the co-holding puzzle — Gross & Souleles (2002); Telyukova (2013)
- to explain why people co-hold — see Section 2; our data cannot answer it
- that hypothetical windfall questions are new or valid on our say-so — Fuster et al. (2021)
- to be first to analyze the 2025 SCF windfall question — Aladangady et al. (2026)
- that families would actually do what they say — the question is hypothetical

**What appears to remain open:**
- **Co-holding and the windfall question.** We did not find a study linking what families say they would do with extra money to whether they already hold cash, among card borrowers. A forward-citation search of Gross & Souleles (2002) turned up none.
- **The debt share.** The Fed's note covers the spending share of the 2025 question; the debt share remains unstudied.
- **Said versus done.** Whether stated allocations match what families actually do.
- **Why.** Precaution, bills, or a "safety" label on savings: the SCF cannot separate them.
- **Advice versus disclosure.** Disclosure changes little (Batista et al.). Whether a conversation with a trusted advisor works differently is untested.
